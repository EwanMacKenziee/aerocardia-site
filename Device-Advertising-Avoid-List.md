# Medical Device Advertising — What You Can't Say / Things to Avoid
### Class II & health/wellness-positioned devices · consumer-directed advertising in Canada

Source: *Guidelines for Consumer Advertising of Health Products* (Ad Standards / Health Canada, 2018 ed.). These guidelines don't have force of law on their own — they sit on top of the **Food and Drugs Act (FDA)**, **Medical Devices Regulations (MDR)**, and **Food and Drug Regulations (FDR)**. Preclearance agencies use them to review consumer ads.

---

## 0. The two laws every device claim hangs on

- **FDA s.20(1):** No advertising of a device that is false, misleading, or deceptive, or likely to create an erroneous impression about its **design, construction, performance, intended use, quantity, character, value, composition, merit, or safety.** This is the umbrella test — every item below is an application of it.
- **The golden rule:** Everything you claim must stay **consistent with the Terms of Market Authorization (TMA)**. For a **Class II** device, the TMA = **the indication(s) for use authorized by Health Canada** (Class III/IV = full Authorized Labelling). You may **paraphrase** the TMA, but you may **not directly or indirectly exceed its scope.**

**Scope note (when these rules don't apply):**
- Devices used *by a healthcare professional* in procedures where the consumer never buys/takes home the device (e.g. laser eye surgery rigs) — out of scope.
- Ads communicating **only the brand name** with **no therapeutic or non-therapeutic claim** — out of scope (e.g. "Brought to you by XYZ").
- Don't advertise a product at all if its TMA has been withdrawn, or it's been discontinued.

---

## 1. Intended use & how the product is represented

| ❌ Avoid | ✅ Rule / Acceptable |
|---|---|
| Visuals, graphics, schematics, or statistics that suggest benefits **beyond** the TMA | Visuals and stats must stay consistent with the TMA; the ad *in its entirety* is judged, not just the words |
| Using footnotes/superscripts to **fix** an otherwise misleading impression | Footnotes may *clarify* or *add* info — must be legible, can't rescue a misleading headline |
| Quotations, journal articles, or study results that **expand** the TMA | Must be consistent with and not exceed the TMA |
| Omitting the intended use entirely | At least communicate the intended use. **Exception:** if intended use is self-evident in the name (e.g. "ACME Hearing Aid", "DENTA Powered Toothbrush"), you may not need to spell it out. A succinct version of the intended use (vs. the full TMA wording) is acceptable. |
| Implying therapeutic claims for one product apply to another in a multi-product/family shot | State indication per product; a general "symptom relief" line is OK only for a true family indicated for a common purpose |

---

## 2. Therapeutic vs. non-therapeutic — keep them separated

Non-therapeutic claims (physical, sensory, market characteristics) are fine, **but they must not imply a therapeutic benefit or obscure the therapeutic indication.**

- ✅ Acceptable **non-therapeutic** device claims (model wording):
  - "You can see your readings in 5 seconds with XYZ Blood Glucose Meter"
  - "24-hour comfort" (contact lenses)
  - "90% of users say ABC Product is easy to use"
  - "#1 selling brand"
- ⚠️ Anything framed as treating/relieving/preventing/killing/fighting/preventing infection is **therapeutic** and must be in the TMA.

---

## 3. Exaggeration & hyperbole (Performance)

| ❌ Can't say | Why |
|---|---|
| "amazing", "powerful", "fantastic" used to **exaggerate** the therapeutic effect | Hyperbole inflating the therapeutic benefit is not allowed |
| The product itself is **"strong"** or **"powerful"** | You *may* say it's "effective", "strong enough", "tough enough", or *has the power to* relieve X — but not that the product is inherently strong/powerful |
| Consumer **"needs"** this specific device/ingredient | You may say someone "needs or wants relief" — not that they need *this product* |
| Implying product use is **essential** | Same principle as above |
| Before/after images that **overstate** real results | Depicted results must accurately reflect what product use actually delivers |
| Overstating the **degree** of relief/benefit | Don't exaggerate how much benefit is obtained |

---

## 4. Timing claims — duration & onset (only if in the TMA)

- **Duration of therapeutic action:** a specific-duration claim ("works for 12 hours") is acceptable **only if it's in the TMA.**
  - Don't equate **dosing interval** with duration of action.
  - Don't equate **pharmacological/pharmacokinetic** duration with **therapeutic** duration unless the TMA says so.
  - If the TMA calls the benefit "temporary," you must say so.
- **Onset of therapeutic action:** a "works in X minutes" claim is acceptable **only if in the TMA.**
  - Don't equate how fast it dissolves/absorbs/responds with time-to-benefit.
  - If results require sustained use over a period, say that accurately (don't imply "quick").
- **Mechanism of action:** must be consistent with the TMA.

---

## 5. "Natural" / "naturally" (relevant if you lean wellness)

- ❌ Don't describe the product's **therapeutic action** as "natural", "natural action", or "acting naturally" — every health product modifies physiology, so this is misleading by definition.
- ❌ Don't imply that "natural"/"naturally sourced" = **safer** than a synthetic alternative.

---

## 6. Strength, guarantees, and "absence of side effect"

- **Guarantees:** ❌ No **therapeutic guarantees.** Don't suggest the device works for *everyone* or *every time*.
  - ✅ "Satisfaction guaranteed, or your money back" (non-therapeutic) is fine.
  - ❌ "Guaranteed relief, or your money back" is not.
- **Absence of side effect:** only claimable under strict conditions (doesn't contradict the TMA side-effect profile; supported by weight of evidence; the side effect is commonly associated with comparable products of the class; no undue emphasis; genuinely useful to consumers).
- **Strength:** efficacy-by-amount claims only if in the TMA.

---

## 7. Health / Healthy / Wellness claims ⭐ (most relevant to a wellness positioning)

Any representation about health, general health, restoration, maintenance, or promotion must be consistent with the TMA. Specifically, advertising must **not**:

- ❌ Suggest the product is a **substitute for good health practices / a healthy lifestyle**
- ❌ Suggest **health will suffer** or that full health can't be achieved **without** the product
- ❌ **Exaggerate the consequences** of not treating a condition
- ❌ **Expand a general health-maintenance claim** into a claim of **specific** health benefits
  - (Drug/NHP illustration of the principle: "maintenance of good health" ✅ → "Promotes heart health" ❌; "supports gut health" ✅ → "for healthy digestion" ❌.) Same logic applies to device wellness messaging: don't escalate a general-wellness authorization into a specific physiological benefit.

---

## 8. Prevention / risk reduction ⭐ (a real gotcha for devices)

- **FDA s.3(1)** prohibits advertising **drugs and medical devices to the general public** as a **treatment, preventative, or cure** for any **Schedule A** disease/disorder/abnormal physical state.
- **Critical:** the exemptions (FDR A.01.067/068; NHPR 103.2/103.3) that let *nonprescription drugs and NHPs* make Schedule A **prevention** claims **do NOT extend to medical devices.** So a Class II device generally **cannot** make Schedule A prevention/treatment/cure claims to consumers.
- Even where prevention/risk-reduction claims are permitted by the TMA, they must **not**:
  - ❌ exaggerate the degree of risk reduction; or
  - ❌ imply the disease risk is **eliminated** (vs. reduced).
- Narrow device carve-outs in the MDR:
  - **Condoms** may be advertised for reducing the **risk** of transmitting STIs — only if the claim is limited to *reducing risk* (s.24(1) MDR).
  - **Contraceptive devices except IUDs** may be advertised for preventing conception, by any means **except** distributing samples door-to-door or by mail (FDA s.3(3); MDR s.24(2)).

## 9. Comparisons

**Therapeutic comparisons** must be consistent with the TMA and not create an erroneous impression. They require:
- a **common authorized indication** between the compared products, compared under the **same conditions of use**;
- **clinical relevance** in humans;
- **conclusive, reproducible, unbiased** evidence to current scientific standards;
- clear identification of the compared products and the relevant indication;
- no unreasonable attack on the competitor.
- ✅ Device model wording: "Healthier gums in 2 weeks vs. Regular Manual Toothbrush B."

**Non-therapeutic comparisons** must be a fair, factual comparison of similar properties, backed by valid, reliable, up-to-date data.
- ✅ Device model wording: "Nine out of 10 patients choose Lancet System X because it is easier to use"; "The highest water content of any soft contact lens for superior comfort"; "Number one best selling dermal filler in Canada."

**"Unique":** must be specific and accurate.
- ❌ Don't claim a unique *therapeutic* effect unless the product genuinely is the only one with that effect/action/formulation.
- ❌ A unique *ingredient* claim can't be stretched into a unique *therapeutic* claim.
- ❌ "Unique" can't rest solely on a difference in active-ingredient level.

---

## 10. Endorsements, seals & Health Canada "approval" ⭐

- **Never** make a **direct or indirect reference to the Act or Regulations** in an ad (FDR C.01.007).
- ❌ Don't state or imply **endorsement, approval, or recommendation by Health Canada** or any government agency.
- ✅ You **may** say **"Product X is authorized for sale by Health Canada"** and include the **Medical Device Licence Number** — *provided the licence has actually been issued.*
- ❌ **Class I** devices (no licence/TMA) **cannot** be represented as "reviewed" or "approved" by Health Canada.
- **Testimonials/UGC:** allowed only if claims stay within the TMA; testimonials must be honest/current opinion; you must hold documentation for any seal; you're responsible for user-generated content on your site staying TMA-consistent.

---

## 11. Safety / "safe" / "side effect free" ⭐

- ❌ Since **all** devices carry **some** risk, you **cannot** suggest a product is **"safe"**, **"side effect free"**, or has **"no known side effects."**
- ❌ Can't suggest "natural"/"naturally sourced" is safer than synthetic.
- ✅ You **must** present **accurate, truthful, balanced** benefit *and* risk information. Ads that emphasize only positives while minimizing real safety concerns are unacceptable. The risk statement must be clearly communicated (visual and/or audio).

### Required risk-communication wording — Medical Devices
| Situation | What the ad must include | Example wording |
|---|---|---|
| Label provides directions for use | Tell consumers to read the label / follow directions | **"Always read and follow the label."** (e.g. powered toothbrushes, blood glucose meters) |
| Use may not suit certain people / some risks identified | + note product may not be suitable for everyone | **"This product may not be right for you. Always read and follow the label."** (e.g. tampons, wart removers, antibacterial bandages) |
| As above **and** use/install/adjust must go through a healthcare professional | + advise discussing suitability with an HCP | **"This product may not be right for you. Talk to your healthcare professional."** (e.g. dermal fillers, contact lenses, hearing aids) |
| Safety advisory issued, label not yet updated | + flag the new risk via another source + point to HCP | **"This product may not be right for you. Always read and follow the label. Talk to your healthcare professional and visit [url] for new product safety information."** |

> **Exempt** from these risk statements: products with no directions for use / no label instructions / no known risks (e.g. SPF lip balms, toothpastes).

**Technical requirements for on-screen risk supers / audio:** stay on screen long enough to be read in full; high-contrast (light-on-dark or dark-on-light), 100% opacity, kerning 0, optional drop shadow; sans-serif font; audio no quieter than the main message; print/OOH/online type size and placement legible to an average consumer.

## 12. Misc. product rules to keep on the checklist

- **Children:** ads must be **overtly directed to adults**; must not suggest a child can self-diagnose/self-medicate or make a rational purchase decision; don't depict storage in places accessible to children.
- **Duration / storage of use:** must match the TMA; don't represent a short-term device for chronic use.
- **Co-packaged products:** represent each component per its own product class; don't let one product's claims bleed onto another.
- **"New / improved / introducing":** usable only for **one year** from first retail sale, and you must specify *what* is new (e.g. "new format").
- **Classification:** represent the device accurately as to its authorized category; don't represent a device as a food or cosmetic.
- **Sampling:** devices are **not** caught by the FDA s.14 drug-sampling ban (drugs/NHPs can't offer free samples to the public; devices generally can) — except the contraceptive-device door-to-door/mail restriction noted in §8.

---

## Quick "never say this" cheat sheet
- "Safe" / "side effect free" / "no known side effects"
- "Powerful" / "strong" product (as an inherent property)
- "Amazing / fantastic" to inflate the therapeutic effect
- "You need this" (vs. "you need relief")
- "Guaranteed relief / results"
- "Approved / endorsed / recommended by Health Canada"
- "Natural action" / "works naturally"
- A specific duration ("for 12 hours") or onset ("in 5 minutes") **not in the TMA**
- Any treatment/preventative/cure claim for a **Schedule A** disease (devices get **no** exemption)
- A specific health benefit dressed up from a general-wellness authorization
- A "unique" therapeutic effect that isn't actually unique
- Any claim — visual, footnote, stat, testimonial, or comparison — that exceeds the **TMA**
